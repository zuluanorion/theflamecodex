Breath, voice and meditation

The Yoga of the Descent · Branch VIII · The word and the Codex

The Descent · the essaysVIII · The word37 / 42
“Once may be enough. Three times may help. A hundred times do not buy Grace.”From Yoga del Descenso · Libro de la Morada

To practice with the Codex you do not need a perfect room, a secret hour or an extraordinary inner state. You need something simpler: enough time not to rush, a body that can take part without violence, and an intention that fits in a sentence.

Start by asking yourself why you are going to practice. "I want to feel something" does not give you enough direction. Phrases like "I need to close the day" or "I want to clarify a decision before acting" let you choose better. Then look at your measure for today. Do you have five minutes or twenty? Are you rested or exhausted? A responsible practice adapts to the person who is here, not to the ideal person the ritual imagined.

And take away the theater you do not need. Dim light, an altar, music or incense can help you gather yourself, but they give a syllable no more authority. If they help you, they serve. If they distract you, set them aside. What is essential still fits in a chair, an ordinary breath and a human voice.

First, listen to the air

Before directing your breath, listen to it. Not to decide whether it is correct, but to know where you are starting from. Perhaps today it is short or deep, irregular, high in the chest, or felt in the belly. That first observation diagnoses nothing. It is your way of greeting the body before asking it to take part.

Posture serves breath and attention; it does not rule them. You can practice sitting, standing or lying down. Your spine can find its order without stiffening: upright does not mean military. And if pain, pressure in the chest, difficulty breathing or any symptom that worries you appears, do not read it as spiritual resistance. Change position, stop, and attend to what needs attending.

Your body does not sign the practice with suffering.

Here I want you to distinguish breath from the Flame. Air is a process of the body. Feeling a fuller inhalation proves no activation, and a small breath does not deny depth. Breath enters the practice because it offers rhythm, voice and transition, not because it measures the Presence. There will be days when the only fitting thing is to change nothing. Before any technique, breath has the right to be breath.

Receive, sustain, express

In the liturgy I propose in the book, the inhalation can represent reception. A comfortable inhalation is enough. The reception the Yoga of the Descent speaks of is consent, not lung capacity.

The pause between inhaling and exhaling can give you an image of sustaining, but in this book the pause is, first of all, natural. I do not teach technical breath retention. The traditions of prāṇāyāma have their own forms, with their contexts and their safeguards, and if you want to study them, you deserve to do so within competent teaching that suits your health. Here, the spontaneous instant when the air stops coming in before it begins to go out is enough. If it does not appear, do not manufacture it. Sustaining a quality does not require holding the air.

The human voice travels naturally on the exhalation, and that is why the sound of the Codex finds its simplest form there. In the basic three-breath form, you pronounce the Core on a comfortable exhalation, Sustaining on the next and Sealing on the third, with an ordinary breath or a brief silence in between. Do not rush for fear of losing what opened. The operation does not depend on speed. And when a syllable's entry indicates something else, a whisper or an inner tone, the entry takes priority.

A voice human enough

You are not looking for a beautiful voice. A rough, small or trembling voice can pronounce with precision, and an impressive voice can distort the name. Full tone is not a shout: it is an audible, natural voice, without tensing the jaw, throat or chest. A whisper can bring the syllable closer to intimacy, but it does not make it more secret or more powerful. And the inner tone lets you work without sound, recalling the syllable clearly, as if you heard it inside.

Pronounce clearly and finish while you are still comfortable. A syllable pronounced clearly in two seconds may serve you better than one sustained with effort for fifteen. The Codex does not reward lung capacity. If you are hoarse, in pain or vocally tired, choose the mental tone or stop. The Flame does not demand that your throat pay the price of the practice. And if a syllable begins to sound inside without your being able to let it rest, makes you anxious or invades your sleep, stop the practice. The word is at your service; you are not at the service of the word.

Where attention rests

You can place your attention on a region, on the axis or on the whole body. Place it the way a hand comes to rest: with presence and without pressing. If you perceive nothing special, the practice is not incomplete. Attention does not need to produce heat, coolness, pressure or current.

The heart holds a central place, because there the Flame is articulated as relationship: Love, Wisdom and Will can recognize one another and learn not to act as enemies. The practice can be very simple: a hand on the breastbone if that is comfortable, natural breathing, the syllable spoken or remembered, a few seconds of silence. The question is not "do I feel the Flame?" but "can these parts of my life feel, see and act from one center?" And if there is pain or pressure in the chest, it is not interpreted first. It is attended to. The symbol knows how to wait.

Between one sound and the next, leave one or two breaths of silence. That space is not an empty pause. It is the place where one function ends before the next begins, and also the place where you may discover that today it is better not to go on. The freedom to stop is part of the safeguard.

Less, closing and returning

There is no universal number that makes a syllable effective. One pronunciation can be enough. More repetitions do not mean more Descent; they can also bring fatigue, hyperventilation, obsession or simply noise. The right measure ends before the mind starts to bargain: "one more, just to be sure."

To close is to return. Your breathing goes back to its own rhythm. You open your eyes, look at the room, move your hands, jaw, shoulders and feet. It may help to drink water, walk a few steps or write a single line, not because these gestures demonstrably discharge energy, but because they bring you back to ordinary life. If an emotion was left open, do not force yourself to resolve it. Name it: "this is still here." And if something feels too intense, set all technical language aside: normal air, open eyes, contact with what surrounds you, the company of someone safe, and professional help when it is called for.

In the hours that follow, observing does not mean keeping watch. There is no need to examine every coincidence or every dream to decide whether the syllable "worked." Look at what is near and human: whether your breathing stayed comfortable, whether the quality you were seeking shows up in some decision, conversation or gesture. Allow, too, for there to be no clear change at all. If you keep a record, keep it brief, and always separate what happened from what you interpret. The word provisional keeps the hand open.

The single syllable keeps its dignity. Silence keeps its dignity. Not practicing keeps its dignity. A spiritual tool shows its maturity when you can leave it on the table without feeling that you have abandoned your life.

FOR YOUR PRACTICE

For one or two minutes, let the air come in and go out on its own. Feel where it moves, without making it bigger.

When you finish, open your eyes, look at the room, and move your hands, shoulders and feet.

Reflect on this: if today you left the syllable on the table, what fear would appear?
