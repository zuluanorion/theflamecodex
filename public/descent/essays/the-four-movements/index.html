The four movements

The Yoga of the Descent · Branch II · Your history and your body

The Descent · the essaysII · History and body06 / 42
“The four movements organize experience. They do not compel it.”From Yoga del Descenso · Libro Puerta

After a time of great inner intensity, or in the middle of it, there sometimes comes a pull toward the concrete. Suddenly you want to walk. To touch the earth. To cook something slowly. To feel your feet on the floor of your house. Your body asks for weight, not lightness. And the most disconcerting part is that this pull toward the simple does not interrupt your inner life. It accompanies it.

Peeling potatoes feels strangely full.

And yet part of you worries. Isn't this a spiritual “step down,” less light and more potatoes? So that this doubt does not make you suffer, I offer you four simple words. I call them the four movements.

A grammar, not a law

Before I introduce them, I want to leave you three warnings, because a useful map becomes dangerous the moment we forget it is a map. The four movements are not stages you have to pass through in order. They are not forces you must feel in order to know you are “doing well.” And they are not a scientific discovery about how the body works. They are the way I propose to put order into responses that would otherwise look like an endless catalogue.

Think of them as the points of the compass. No one is obliged to walk north, and no journey uses all four directions equally. But whoever knows the points of the compass can describe which way what they are living seems to be heading, tell others about it, and notice when it changed course. That is what I offer you: not an itinerary, but a way to orient yourself and to tell your own story.

Downward and toward the center

The first is the movement that the image of “going up” taught us to misunderstand. The downward movement gathers those moments when your attention and your life turn toward the concrete, toward the body, the ground and rootedness, when what you received stops staying in your head and in prayer and begins to be felt in concrete life, in the body and in the earth. The temptation to correct it is strong, because it seems less spiritual. But taking root is not going down a level. It can be as simple as walking, cooking, resting or doing something with your hands. You are not abandoning the path. You are letting the path reach the floor.

The second is the quietest, and for that very reason the one we trample most. After something intense, a deep experience, a conversation that moved you, a season of great openness, there comes a strange wish to do nothing with it yet. You do not want to tell it. You do not want to analyze it. You want silence, alone or in quiet company, like someone who has just eaten something substantial and needs to linger at the table. That is the movement toward the center: something in you gathers itself in to be digested and integrated. It is a digestion that cannot be seen, cannot be hurried and is not done by effort, and within it, even when there is nothing new to tell, integration may be taking place.

If it comes, protect it. It is the movement modern life respects least: we receive something deep and within the hour we post it, comment on it, turn it into content. Sharing is not forbidden, but notice the difference between sharing from what has already settled and spilling over from the fizz. When the wish to linger at the table comes, take it. The silence afterward is part of the meal.

Through the whole body

The third movement is the hardest to describe, and when it happens you recognize it very well. Something that began in one place, a warmth in the chest or a stillness in the belly, stops having an address. It is not that your chest is at peace; it is that you are at peace, from head to toe, like a house where there is no longer a single lit room but a whole morning. It is usually discreet. It does not arrive with trumpets but with a quiet evenness, and many people only notice it afterward, looking back: “I realized I had been like this all afternoon.”

Here I ask two kinds of care of you. A diffuse discomfort that persists is not interpreted; you take it to a doctor. And this movement is not manufactured by spreading the sensation with your imagination. If something becomes general, it does so on its own. Your work is the same as always: not to get in the way.

Upward and outward

The fourth closes the circle. New wishes appear: to say, to do, to sing while you clean, to write to someone, to offer yourself for something. It is not the fizz of telling the experience while it is still hot. It is something else, calmer, more available, with less urgency and more to spare. It is the movement upward and outward: what has been integrated begins to find expression. I note it as something that sometimes happens, not as a sequence.

Expression that is born from what has been integrated can usually wait. It needs no urgency, no grandiosity, and it does not need you to suddenly become someone with a mission. It is domestic: your voice that sounds different, decisions that come out cleaner, a new patience with your own people, the boundaries you finally speak without a fight, the hand that offers itself without weariness in everyday service. Its full version has its own Part later in the book. For now it is enough that you recognize its face.

What they are for, and what they are not

They are for describing yourself. “These days there is a lot of downward” is a complete sentence, useful and honest. It lets you understand your moment without diagnosing it, and tell someone you trust without half an hour of explanations. A shared vocabulary is one of the simplest ways of not being alone with all this.

They are for not being frightened. The season of potatoes and ground stops looking like a spiritual crisis. The silent lingering at the table stops looking like dryness.

But they are not for grading yourself. No movement is higher. There is no right order. Several may appear in a single day, one may dominate for months, there may be seasons when you recognize none, and none of that says anything about the depth of your process. Whoever turns the four movements into a ladder of progress has broken them.

Nor are they for proving anything. The movements put order into the responses of your house; they do not certify the light. I leave it to you in one line:

A direction describes an experience; it does not prove its cause.

If one day you feel none of them, that does not allow you to conclude that the dawn came any less.

FOR YOUR PRACTICE

Ask yourself which of these four directions you recognize in your days right now: downward, toward the center, through the whole body, upward and outward.

Did you want to correct or hurry one of them because it did not seem spiritual enough?

And what changes if, instead of grading it, you simply describe it?
