The pathways of response and the nāḍīs

The Yoga of the Descent · Branch VII · Grace and the living body

The Descent · the essaysVII · Grace and body31 / 42
“A way of response does not carry the Presence. It shows where a life is responding.”From Yoga del Descenso · Libro de la Morada

Imagine you are sitting in silence and your breathing deepens on its own. You did not push it. It simply became slower, deeper. And right away comes the temptation to explain it with big words: my currents came into balance, a channel opened, something rose up my spine. In the book I offer you another sentence, much simpler and truer: “my breathing deepened while I was in silence.”

That simplicity is not poverty. It is the first gesture of this Part, which keeps a promise I left you in the Libro Puerta, the Door Book.

A promise left open

In the first volume I called pathways of response certain human functions in which an experience becomes visible with particular clarity: breathing, seeing, listening, speaking, assimilating, bonding and generating, letting go, and standing in your axis. I deliberately presented them in everyday words. I did not speak to you of hidden channels, nor ask you to imagine pipes inside you. I told you something more modest: a way of response is not a place in your body; it is a function of your life.

At the end of that Part a question remained open. Centuries earlier, the yoga traditions had developed maps of a subtle anatomy in which the nāḍīs appear. The resemblance could be studied; the equivalence was not to be presumed. Now we can go in, because we have the distinctions that were missing then.

The promise was not to find an identity. It was to learn to compare without erasing.

Each word in its own house

Nāḍī is a word from the traditions of India, usually translated as channel, conduit or current within subtle anatomy. There is no single ancient chart that everyone copied without variation: there are families of maps, born in concrete contexts of practice. Three names return again and again with special importance: Iḍā, Piṅgalā and Suṣumṇā. The first two usually represent lateral currents; the third, a central axis.

All of this belongs to a subtle body. A nāḍī is not a nerve that ought to show up in a dissection. If you translate it as nerve so that it sounds scientific, you lose the traditional map; if you present it as proven physical anatomy, you lose honesty.

And there is an even more important distinction for us. Prāṇa is not breath, even though in practice the two are very close. And the Flame is not prāṇa. In the doctrine of this book, the Flame is the manifested essence of the Monad; prāṇa belongs to the vital plane. Confusing them would reduce the divine essence to fuel and turn spirituality into the engineering of currents.

Verbs and highways

When you try to build a table, the temptation appears: breathing should correspond to Iḍā and Piṅgalā, speaking to a channel in the throat, assimilating to a digestive nāḍī. The table fills up and it looks as if we have proven something. In reality, perhaps we have only placed different words in parallel columns. That is why I go through the ways one by one, and the comparison gradually shows its real shape.

Breathing is the way that converses most visibly with the nāḍīs. Many prāṇāyāma practices work with the breath, and sometimes with the alternation of the nostrils, in relation to Iḍā and Piṅgalā. That relationship is real within their systems. But air is not prāṇa, and a breath that deepens does not prove that the Flame is traveling through a channel. The Flame does not need to be pumped. Breathing can be a door; it is not proof.

Standing in your axis finds the clearest correspondence with Suṣumṇā, because both images are axial. And precisely because the likeness is strong, it calls for more care, not less. The spinal cord is not Suṣumṇā, and Suṣumṇā is not the Flame. The axis has primacy, but not a monopoly: the heart, the pelvis, the legs, the breath and ordinary life do not become secondary zones because a center exists.

Speaking, assimilating and letting go, on the other hand, converse more with the vāyus, the directions or functions of the movement of prāṇa, such as udāna, samāna and apāna, than with any single nāḍī. This finding does not get in the way. It makes the comparison more precise. It shows us that we were comparing verbs with highways, and that the traditional map has verbs of its own.

Seeing and listening, finally, do not have enough grounds to be assigned exclusively to any of the three. That cell stays open. An honest system is not ashamed to write “no direct correspondence.”

What polarity cannot decide

In many descriptions, Iḍā is linked to the Moon, coolness and receptivity; Piṅgalā, to the Sun, heat and activity. As images they can illuminate a great deal: breathing without pushing, listening before answering, and also focusing, acting and giving form. A mature gaze needs both gestures.

But here polarity needs an explicit protection. A woman “is” not Iḍā; a man “is” not Piṅgalā. Every person receives and acts, listens and expresses, contains and directs. Iḍā is not the parasympathetic nervous system or one half of the brain, and Piṅgalā is not a synonym for domination. Using subtle anatomy to lock someone into a gender or a role betrays both human complexity and the complexity of the traditional map.

Nor is it a tool for diagnosis. “Your Iḍā is blocked,” “your Piṅgalā dominates,” “Suṣumṇā didn't open”: such sentences sound like knowledge and trade an open experience for a verdict the person may begin to obey. Coolness on the left does not prove Iḍā. Warmth on the right does not prove Piṅgalā. The body does not hand you labels along with its sensations.

Inhabiting, not opening

The practical law of the Libro Puerta still stands: the ways are not opened; they are inhabited. Seeing is not activated; you learn to see with presence. Listening is not unblocked with an affirmation; you listen more honestly. And listening inwardly does not mean obeying everything that appears. What speaks to you inside is not exempt from being tested just because it speaks softly.

Something similar happens with letting go. Not everything has to be released, and not every emotion needs a catharsis. Forcing tears, trembling or the breath does not guarantee integration; it can produce overwhelm. When letting go is real, it often looks less like expelling and more like ceasing to obey: a habit is left with no work to do, an inner sentence stops governing you.

Letting go is not tearing out. Often it is ceasing to hold.

And the body keeps its priority. Shortness of breath, pain, fainting or persistent changes in breathing are attended to clinically. If insistent voices or commands appear, ones that terrify you or that you find hard to tell apart from shared reality, do not romanticize them as the opening of a channel: seek professional support soon. A subtle map can wait. Oxygen cannot.

In the end, what this Part leaves you is a way of looking. Breathing is still breathing, with all its depth. The nāḍī is still a nāḍī, with all its history. Two maps can meet precisely because each one arrives at the meeting with its own name.

FOR YOUR PRACTICE

Reflect on this: is there something you want to tear off yourself?

What would change if, instead of tearing it off, you stopped holding it?

And if something in you answers “not yet,” can you listen to that too, without fighting it?
