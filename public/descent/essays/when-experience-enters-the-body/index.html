When spiritual experience enters the body

The Yoga of the Descent · Branch I · The Door

The Descent · the essaysI · The Door02 / 42
“Having an experience is a moment. Being transformed by it is a life.”From Yoga del Descenso · Libro Puerta

Perhaps you know this closely. A retreat that changed your life for two weeks. A prayer in which you understood everything with a clarity that was not your own, and then, on an ordinary Tuesday a month later, you found yourself shouting at someone in traffic as if that clarity had never existed. A moment of peace so deep that you thought “nothing will ever be the same again,” and yet almost everything went back to being the same.

The conclusion that offers itself is bitter, and it turns against you: “it wasn’t real,” or “I didn’t know how to keep it,” or “I’m too weak for this.”

I propose another conclusion:

What you lived does not have to have been false just because it did not stay.

What you lived was real as an experience. What may have been missing was not intensity, but time for integration. I call incarnation the process by which a spiritual experience begins to change, in a more stable way, the life that receives it. And that process has its own timing, which is not the timing of the moment that set it in motion.

The house and the guest

Imagine your life as a house. Not an ideal house: yours. With its tidy rooms and its drawers you never open, with rooms full of light and the odd door you would rather not touch. Built over decades by your parents, your history, your fears, your habits, your loves and your losses. A lived-in house.

Now imagine that one afternoon a luminous guest arrives. She comes in without forcing anything, and while she is there the whole house feels different: light enters rooms that had been closed for years, the air changes, and even the furniture seems to be at peace. Then the visit ends, and the house feels like the house it always was.

Hear this without reproach: that does not mean the guest was a lie. It means something much simpler. A visit can fill a house with light, but making it habitable for that light is another process. For the visit to become a dwelling, the house would have to become capable of holding that presence: opening the closed rooms, repairing what creaks, making room. And that does not happen in one afternoon, however beautiful the afternoon was.

A moment and a life

The moment can last a few seconds and be completely real. Your life, on the other hand, your character, your reactions, the way you sleep, argue, wait and forgive, was built over decades, and it has its own weight, its own inertia and its own loyalties.

When the experience and the life meet, they do not simply merge. They look at each other. And between them begins a long, silent negotiation that almost no one described to you: the experience asks for room, and the life, which is made of habit, defends the shape it already knows. Sometimes neither has to defeat the other. Transformation may consist precisely in the new finding a human way to stay.

That is why a person can have touched something very high and still react from the same old place. It is not hypocrisy. It is not failure. It is the natural distance between a moment and a life, and that distance does not close by piling up moments, however intense they are. What decides is what your life manages to integrate from them. It matters less how many times it happens than how much of what happened can begin to live with you. Slowly. Downward.

Your body is faithful

Of all the layers of your life, the one that takes longest to change is the most honest one: your body. The mind can be convinced in an afternoon and the emotions can be moved in an instant, but the body is neither convinced nor moved. The body learns, and it learns the way it learned everything it knows: by repetition, with time, at its own pace.

Think of everything your body has already learned without asking your permission. To tense up when a certain voice rises. To shrink before certain memories. To breathe short when there is conflict. To brace for the blow even though the blow stopped coming twenty years ago. You did not decide any of that; you practiced it without knowing, thousands of times, until it became flesh and turned into your way of being.

Your body is not stubborn out of malice: it is faithful. Faithful to what the years taught it. And when something appears that it did not know, a new peace, a stillness it is not used to, it may grow quiet, tense up, tremble, change its breathing, or simply go back to what it knows. Not all of those responses have a spiritual meaning, and that is why it is wise to observe them before interpreting them.

This rearranges everything. Your body is not lagging behind your spirit. It moves at the speed at which flesh learns, and that speed is not a flaw in the process. It is the process. A powerful experience can happen in seconds; digesting it can take days, months or years. No one told you this, and perhaps you have spent a long time measuring yourself with the wrong yardstick.

The waves are its breathing

There is something else you probably noticed and no one explained: this does not move in a straight line. There are weeks of a peace that seems final, and weeks when everything old comes back with a force that frightens you. And once again the bitter conclusion offers itself: “I’m going backward.”

Go back to the house. No one renovates an entire house all at once while still living in it. One room is renovated, and while that work lasts there is dust, noise and things out of place; then a rest, and then the next room. Seen from inside, in the middle of the dust, it looks like chaos. Seen from outside, it is exactly how a lived-in house is transformed. A total change all at once would look more like a demolition, and you need to go on living, working and caring for your own while the work moves forward.

Relapses are not always what they seem either. Sometimes a difficult period coincides with old material becoming visible again, and then it can be worked with. Other times it is tiredness, stress, grief or anxiety, something that needs another kind of attention. There is no rule that lets you know this just from how intensely you feel it. For now I ask only this of you: do not be in a hurry to name the wave.

Where to look

Everything above flows into a single sentence, and for me it is the most important one on this stretch of the path. Read it twice:

The goal is not to chase the experience. The goal is to become capable of holding what transforms.

Whoever wants to repeat the experience lives backward, comparing every prayer with that prayer and every silence with that silence, and their spiritual life turns into the pursuit of a memory. Whoever understands the second goal lives forward, and downward. They no longer ask heaven “when will you come back?” but ask their house “which room is next?” They stop collecting moments and begin to prepare a dwelling. And when you stop chasing the experience and devote yourself to becoming capable of it, the experience stops being something rare, because now there is somewhere to receive it.

No one is asking you to achieve this. I am showing you where to look.

One morning you may feel a peace so deep that you think something has changed forever. At lunchtime someone spills a glass on the table, and you react exactly as you always have. The morning’s peace was not a lie. The midday reaction was not one either. Between the two lies the work.

FOR YOUR PRACTICE

Ask yourself what yardstick you have been measuring yourself with.

How would you look at your body if, instead of reproaching it for its slowness, you saw it as someone faithful who is learning something new?

And when a wave comes, do not rush to name it while you are still inside it.
