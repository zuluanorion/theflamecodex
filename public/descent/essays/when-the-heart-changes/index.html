When the heart changes

The Yoga of the Descent · Branch V · Toward others

The Descent · the essaysV · Toward others17 / 42
“This change does not make saints. It softens people.”From Yoga del Descenso · Libro Puerta

Perhaps it has already happened to you. The person who used to do nothing but irritate you one day stirs tenderness in you, and you could not say why, because nothing obvious has changed in them. The same old argument comes around and you discover you no longer need to win it. Someone offers you help and, for the first time in years, you accept it without negotiating. You listen to your son, your mother, your partner, and there is a moment, perhaps two seconds long, when you are not preparing your answer. You are simply there, with that person. And it is enough.

This is something many people did not go looking for and found anyway. At some point along the way, sometimes without having decided it and without any technique aimed at bringing it about, the way you love begins to move. It does not always arrive like this: love is also cultivated on purpose, and that path is just as worthy. But when it comes without your seeking it, that is precisely what surprises you. It does not feel like an achievement. It feels like a change in the ground beneath you.

I want to ask you just one question: what happens when everything that has been growing clearer inside you begins to change the way you love the person in front of you? Because what became embodied does not stay inside. It can begin to become relationship.

What changes, and what does not

It is not so much who you love, or how much, that changes. It is the how. Love becomes less conditional, less “I love you if” and more “I love you and”. There is less need for the other person to be a certain way in order to be loved. There is less bookkeeping, that silent ledger of what was given and what is owed that turns so many loves into treasuries. And something harder to put into words happens: the other begins to appear as other. No longer as a character in your story, nor as the supplier of what you lack, but as someone who exists whole in their own right. And seeing them that way, far from pushing them away, brings them closer.

Put in a single line, you begin to love less from fear: of losing, of not measuring up, of being left in debt, of not being enough.

Now, this needs looking after right away. A less conditional love does not mean that access to your life becomes unconditional. You can love someone and not live with them. You can understand them and not trust them with the same things. You can stop hating them and still keep telling them no. Loving more does not mean allowing more.

And to take the illusion apart before it does harm, let me also tell you what does not change. You are still you, with your character, your history and your thresholds. You still argue, perhaps better, but you argue. You still have days when your heart is closed, and that does not disprove what has begun, because losing days is not losing the process. You do not love everyone equally, and you do not have to. This change does not make saints. It softens people.

Receiving, the hard half

There is an imbalance that is hardly ever mentioned: many people know how to give long before they know how to receive.

You can give from many places, including the old kitchen of patterns: giving so as not to owe, giving to make sure, giving to earn a place. Someone who has pleased others all their life can give in their sleep. Receiving touches different territory, because sometimes it means letting yourself be seen in need, setting down for a moment the role of the one who can manage alone, and allowing the other to reach you.

That is why, for many people, one of the deepest changes of this station is not in becoming more generous. It is in the first time they accept help without apologizing, a compliment without handing it straight back, care without feeling it as a debt. And learning to receive does not mean receiving everything. There is help you accept, offers you are grateful for, and doors you leave closed.

And notice that the Descent, from the very first page, is the story of learning to receive: the Presence, peace, whatever arrives. Sometimes that learning finds a very human echo, and the same heart that learned to let itself be reached by the Presence also learns to let itself be reached by another person’s embrace, help or care.

Limits without closing the heart, forgiveness without abandoning yourself

These two twin laws correct the same misunderstanding: the belief that a changed heart no longer says no.

A limit is not the opposite of love. You can say no with an open heart, and sometimes that no is precisely the act of love, toward yourself and toward the other too, because a false yes neither loves them nor helps them. Saying no to what harms you is health, not hardness. And a limit does not need hatred to be firm. Sometimes it allows closeness. Sometimes it demands distance. And sometimes, out of care and for safety, it means closing a relationship completely.

An open heart does not oblige you to keep any door open.

That sentence has a twin: loving someone does not give them access to you.

And forgiveness is not abandoning yourself. Forgiving, when it comes and if it comes, is not forgetting, nor declaring that it did not hurt, nor going back, nor exposing yourself again, and it does not require reconciliation either. You can forgive someone and never see them again. Nor can forgiveness be manufactured. Forgiveness forced out of spiritual duty is another way of betraying yourself, and it usually lasts as long as the performance does. “Not yet” is also an answer on the path, and with forgiveness it is often the most honest one for years.

I will tell you something more, very plainly: healing does not depend on your managing to forgive someday. There are processes whose honest form is not forgiveness but ceasing to live organized around the harm. And if the forgiveness of this station stands out in any way, it is that when it comes without your forcing it, it does not cost you your dignity.

Tenderness and its impostor

This station has an impostor, and you probably know it already: people-pleasing in a new disguise.

From the outside they look very much alike. Inside they are organized differently. People-pleasing tends to organize itself around fear, and it gives in so that nothing happens: no conflict, no abandonment, no discomfort. I do not say this to condemn anyone, because many defenses began as solutions. Tenderness, when it is free, leaves you more room to choose. It can say the difficult no, have the conversation that burns, disappoint someone you love, and do all of that without closing the heart.

Sometimes the learning goes wrong. Someone tries to set a limit calmly, ends up saying it as a reproach, and the conversation breaks. The next day no revelation arrives. A humbler sentence does:

“Yesterday I had something true to say and I said it badly. I’m sorry.”

The heart is not proven by never failing. Sometimes it shows itself in the capacity to come back, without turning the repair into theater.

That impostor has a more abstract cousin: universal love that touches no one. You can feel an oceanic love for humanity and still be unable to have lunch with your own brother. That wide experience can be beautiful and real, but it does not replace the way we treat the actual people we share our lives with. It is easier to love humanity when humanity is not sitting across from you in the kitchen.

When the other no longer feels entirely other

Some people describe something deeper, and I gather it here without certifying it and without dismissing it. These are moments that are almost always brief and almost always ordinary: listening to someone, watching a child sleep, keeping someone company in their pain. For a moment the everyday separation does not tell the whole story. You are still you and the other is still other, but the distance feels less absolute. More than euphoria, it tends to leave a quiet tenderness and a wish to care.

This is not fusion. Losing your own limits, or living someone else’s life instead of your own, is its dangerous caricature, and what it usually needs is help, not celebration. An experience of unity does not cancel difference, or consent, or limits, or the other person’s sovereignty. And a love that asks you to disappear does not belong to this station. If a relationship is gradually isolating you from others, erasing your limits or charging you your identity as the price of belonging, that is an alarm, and you talk it over with people you trust and, if needed, with professional help. Seeking professional help does not interrupt your spiritual path.

A more sensitive heart can also be more exposed to grief and to the pain of others. That is not a mistake in the process. You accompany it with the basics, with company, and without demanding that you endure everything.

Because in the end, a changed heart becomes visible in the way you treat the person in front of you: this week, with these people, in this house.

FOR YOUR PRACTICE

When you notice you are growing softer with someone, ask yourself: can you still say no? Do you have more capacity to choose, or less and less?

Is the peace in your relationships peace, or is it bought silence?

And notice what happens in you when someone offers you help, a compliment or care: can you receive it without apologizing and without feeling it as a debt?
