When what you received begins to speak through you

The Yoga of the Descent · Branch V · Toward others

The Descent · the essaysV · Toward others19 / 42
“Speak of your own path; do not legislate anyone else’s.”From Yoga del Descenso · Libro Puerta

That conversation you finally know how to have, the one that had been waiting its turn for years. A new way of doing the same work. A decision that puts your life in order and needs no explaining. A page written for no one. A meal cooked a different way. A way of keeping someone company in their grief. A service no one sees, which something in you needed to give.

Sometimes, not always, not for everyone and not as a destiny, what you have understood begins to ask for a form in your life. And almost every time we hear the word “expression,” a single image is smuggled in with it: someone talking about spiritual matters in front of other people. That is one form, but not the only one, and in many lives it is not even the one that describes them best.

Through you, in your everyday voice

Before going on, I want to look after the title of this essay, because it lends itself to misunderstanding. When I say that what you received “begins to speak through you,” I am not saying that some entity takes over your voice, or that you must obey something speaking through you. I am saying something much more human: that what you have lived can begin to find form in your words, your decisions, your creations and your way of serving. Through you means through your human life, with your everyday voice, your everyday vocabulary and your everyday hands.

Not every expression needs an audience. There are deep expressions that happen whole in ordinary life, with no one watching and no need to explain where they come from. If what you received shows first in how you treat others and in what you decide, and that is where it shows, then life itself is the first work. Words, when they come, can come later, and they are not obliged to come at all. There are whole paths, deep and complete, that expressed themselves without saying a single sentence about themselves.

And the idea I want to protect here, the one everything else hangs from, fits in four words: expressing is not imposing. Having received something does not give you authority to turn it into a decree about someone else’s life.

When to speak, and when the table still rules

What is said is shared from what has settled, not spilled out from the fizz of excitement. And this station adds something finer: knowing how to discern the right time.

There are inner states that invite you to wait before speaking. The urgency to convince, the need for the other person to confirm what you say so that you can believe it yourself, the urge to steer every conversation straight toward your own experience: these are good reasons to pause. That is when the sobremesa, the long unhurried talk at the table after a meal, still rules. You listen more and say less.

And there are moments when speaking can come more simply: when someone has truly asked you, when what you want to say answers the conversation you are actually having, when you can offer it without needing it to be accepted, and when you can stay quiet afterward. A word that is offered is let go. Whatever the other does with it, even nothing, no longer belongs to you.

Before you open your mouth, there is a simple way to examine it. Ask yourself: does this word answer a real question from the other person, or a need of mine? No answer is a certificate, but that question, asked in time, saves years of sermons. And it has a clear limit: it applies when you are speaking directly with someone. To write a page, make a work, teach a class or begin a service, no one needs to have asked you first.

The grammar of the first person

The difference between expressing and imposing is not only in intensity or subject. It can also be recognized in the stance you speak from: whether you offer or decree, whether you leave room for disagreement, whether you acknowledge the limits of what you know, whether you respect the other person’s no.

There is a grammar that protects that stance, the grammar of the first person: this is what I lived, this is what helped me, this is how I understand it today. Opposite it stands the grammar of decree: this is how it is, you should, everyone needs. The first offers the other a territory of yours to look at if they wish. The second legislates over someone else’s territory. Speak of your own path; do not legislate anyone else’s.

Three very practical consequences follow. Expression respects the other’s no, including lack of interest, which is the most common form of that no and the hardest to accept. The people you love have every right to find none of this interesting, and your love for them does not depend on converting them. Expression can bear questions: if what you say cannot withstand a “and how do you know that?” without your needing to defend it, there is good reason to look again at how you are holding it. And responsible expression does not try to manufacture dependence. If your way of sharing begins to make your approval or your presence a condition for the other trusting their own judgment, there is serious reason to look at how you are occupying that place.

The four traps

There are four traps, and all four turn expression into something else.

The pulpit is every after-dinner conversation turned into a lecture, every exchange steered toward the subject, every silence from the other read as an invitation. Its cost is very homely: people begin to filter what they tell you. Mature expression knows how to talk about football.

The salesman is needing the other to buy, to try it, to believe it, to convert, so that you can sustain your own faith. When you need others to agree with you, perhaps you are no longer only sharing what you received. Your need for confirmation is speaking too, through a megaphone.

Rank is letting expression become identity, and identity become hierarchy: the one who knows, the one who guides, the one who arrived. What you received does not make you special; if anything, it makes you responsible. And the responsibility of the one who expresses is the exact opposite of rank: that the other keeps what was said, not the one who said it.

And the fourth is the word bigger than life: speaking from further along than you have actually come, teaching what you have read as if you had lived it, promising what you have not tried. It is the quietest trap, because it usually begins with generosity. You are not forbidden to teach what you studied or to pass on what you learned from others; you are forbidden to switch the labels. You can share what you studied as studied, what you received as received, a hypothesis as a hypothesis and a question as a question. What you cannot do is sell the question as settled.

Your own voice

There is something that can help you find your way: still recognizing yourself in the voice you use.

When what you received finds form, it usually finds it in your voice: in your vocabulary, your humor, your kitchen examples. Sometimes, when we talk about spiritual things, a character shows up in us, a solemnity, a jargon or a way of speaking we use nowhere else. That does not make the experience false. But ask yourself whether that way of speaking helps you express yourself honestly, or whether it is starting to trade your own voice for a role.

Expressing has real costs. There are workplaces, families and communities where speaking about this costs dearly, and choosing silence there is not betrayal. It is discerning the time. If expression becomes compulsive, grandiose or inseparable from a mission that isolates you, you stop, you talk it over, you consult.

And when you express, others may begin to bring you their pain. Listening is a gift you can give; treating is not. Accompanying is not treating, and serving is not saving. If someone comes to you with something that overwhelms them, whether a crisis, a harm or despair, the most loving thing your words can do is help them reach someone who knows how to help. Suggesting professional help does not interrupt anyone’s spiritual path, and sometimes it is the best way to accompany it.

Integration does not need a spiritual character.

The deepest words of this path have often been spoken with an apron on.

FOR YOUR PRACTICE

Before you speak about your path with someone, ask yourself: does this word answer a real question from the other person, or a need of mine?

If you speak, use the grammar of the first person: this is what I lived, this is what helped me, this is how I understand it today.

And then let the word go. Whatever the other does with it, even nothing, no longer belongs to you.
